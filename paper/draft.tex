\title{Exact block reductions in Fourier-modal scattering theory for layered periodic media}

\author[1]{N. V. Popov}

\begin{abstract}
The Fourier modal method represents Maxwell's equations in a periodic layer by
a finite set of $n$ reciprocal-lattice harmonics.  In a generic formulation,
the modal problem has dimension $2n$ and the stable composition of layers by a
Redheffer product requires dense cubic algebra.  We derive exact reductions of
these operators for structures occurring frequently in diffraction theory.
For an isotropic homogeneous layer, a permutation to the per-harmonic basis
$(E_{x,h},E_{y,h})$ decomposes the Maxwell operator into independent
$2\times2$ blocks at arbitrary incidence.  Consequently, an arbitrary
homogeneous multilayer is propagated in $\order(nN_{\mathrm{u}})$ operations,
where $N_{\mathrm{u}}$ is the number of homogeneous interfaces.  For a
$y$-invariant patterned layer, the modal matrix is block diagonal at normal
incidence and block lower triangular under conical illumination.  Its spectrum
is therefore obtained from two $n\times n$ non-Hermitian eigenproblems; in the
conical case we give an explicit reconstruction of the coupled eigenvectors.
We also derive a block contraction for quadratic volume observables that avoids
the $4n\times4n$ intermediate matrices of the direct expression.  Numerical
experiments against grcwa and S4 verify the scattering observables and Fourier
fields to the accuracy of the reference discretization.  For an extreme-
ultraviolet absorber on a 40-period Mo/Si mirror, the homogeneous-stack
reduction accelerates the scattering stage by approximately $3.4$--$3.7$,
while the combined one-dimensional reductions give an order-of-magnitude or
greater acceleration for line gratings.  The analysis separates exact
operator reductions from approximation-dependent convergence acceleration and
identifies the patterned-layer eigensystem as the remaining asymptotic
bottleneck.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Fourier modal method; rigorous coupled-wave
analysis; diffraction gratings; scattering matrix; conical incidence;
block-triangular eigenproblem.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Rigorous coupled-wave analysis (RCWA), also called the Fourier modal method
(FMM), is the workhorse for computing the optical response of layered,
laterally periodic photonic structures: diffraction gratings, photonic
crystals, thin-film solar cells, metasurfaces, and the phase-shifting absorber
stacks of extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) lithography
\cite{moharam1981rcwa,moharam1995formulation,liu2014s4}.
The method expands the electromagnetic field in a truncated set of $n$
planewave (Fourier) harmonics in the plane of periodicity, reduces Maxwell's
equations in each laterally structured layer to a $2n\times2n$ non-Hermitian
eigenproblem, and propagates the modal amplitudes across the stack with a
scattering-matrix (Redheffer) recursion \cite{whittaker1999scattering,li1996recursive}.
Reflectivity, transmissivity, per-order diffraction efficiencies, near-field
intensities, and absorbed-power distributions all follow from the assembled
$S$-matrix and the per-layer modal data.

The numerical difficulty is controlled by the number $n$ of retained spatial
harmonics.  A patterned layer requires a non-Hermitian eigenproblem of order
$2n$, while stable composition across interfaces requires dense matrix products
and linear solves of the same order.  Both stages scale cubically and store
$\order(n^2)$ complex numbers.  Large-period gratings make this cost especially
severe: at wavelength $\lambda$, a period $L_x$ admits on the order of
$2L_x/\lambda$ propagating diffraction channels before evanescent harmonics are
included.  Repeated evaluations in wavelength, angle, or geometric parameter
studies compound the cost.

A generic implementation spends its time in two algebraic operations:

\begin{enumerate}
\item $O\big((2n)^3\big)$ for the (generally non-Hermitian, complex)
  eigendecomposition \eqref{eq:eig} of each laterally structured layer, and
\item $O\big((2n)^3\big)$ dense matrix products and inversions \emph{per layer
  interface} in the Redheffer recursion \eqref{eq:star}.
\end{enumerate}

As a representative geometry, consider
vacuum$\,|\,$absorber$\,|\,$cap$\,|\,40\times$(Mo/Si)$\,|\,$substrate.  The
single patterned layer is followed by approximately 80 homogeneous interfaces.
Treating those interfaces as generic dense scatterers repeats cubic work even
though translational invariance forbids coupling between distinct in-plane
wavevectors in every homogeneous layer.  The purpose of this paper is to expose
that invariance, and related invariant subspaces of the patterned operator,
directly in the finite-dimensional Maxwell system.

\subsection{Context and main results}

The Fourier-modal method was introduced for diffraction gratings by Moharam and
Gaylord \cite{moharam1981rcwa} and refined into a stable, efficient formulation
for binary gratings in \cite{moharam1995formulation}. The stability of the
layer-recursion step was settled by Li's comparison of the $R$-, $T$-, and
$S$-matrix algorithms \cite{li1996recursive}, establishing the scattering-matrix
(Redheffer) formulation as the method of choice for thick and lossy stacks; our
work uses exactly this recursion \cite{redheffer1962transmission}. The
convergence of the modal expansion for discontinuous media was analyzed by Li
\cite{li1996fourier}, whose inverse rule (and its generalization, the
normal-vector field / fast Fourier factorization \cite{li1997formulation})
removes the algebraic convergence bottleneck of the naive Laurent rule for
high-contrast metallic gratings. The scattering-matrix treatment of patterned
multilayer photonic structures was systematized by Whittaker and Culshaw
\cite{whittaker1999scattering}.

Convergence acceleration by Fourier factorization and normal-vector fields
reduces the harmonic order needed at a specified accuracy
\cite{li1996fourier,li1997formulation}.  The present work addresses a different
question: given a fixed Fourier discretization, which parts of its algebra are
exactly reducible?  The principal results are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A homogeneous isotropic layer is a direct sum of $n$ two-dimensional
  polarization spaces, for any reciprocal-lattice truncation and any Bloch
  wavevector.  A homogeneous stack therefore admits an exact per-harmonic
  scattering recursion.
\item For a $y$-invariant isotropic profile, the patterned modal matrix splits
  into two $n$-dimensional spectral problems.  At normal incidence the matrix
  is block diagonal; at conical incidence it is block lower triangular, and
  the off-diagonal eigenvector block is obtained from a Sylvester equation.
\item Quadratic volume observables can be contracted using $2n$-dimensional
  blocks, without constructing the direct $4n$-dimensional expression.
\end{enumerate}

These are identities of the discretized Maxwell operator.  They introduce no
material, weak-coupling, or paraxial approximation and are complementary to
factorization rules that improve convergence with $n$.

The open-source solvers grcwa \cite{grcwa} and S4 \cite{liu2014s4} are used as
independent numerical references.  The derivation is developed in
\cref{sec:formulation,sec:uniform,sec:eig}; implementation and numerical tests
are reported in \cref{sec:impl,sec:results}.

\section{Fourier-modal formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

\subsection{Geometry, harmonics, and notation}

Consider a stack of layers, each invariant along $z$ (the propagation axis),
with the in-plane permittivity $\varepsilon(x,y)$ periodic on a lattice with
direct vectors $\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2$ and reciprocal vectors
$\mathbf{K}_1,\mathbf{K}_2$. The field is expanded in the $n$ retained
harmonics
$\{\mathbf{G}_i\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\{\,m_1\mathbf{K}_1+m_2\mathbf{K}_2\}$,
so that every in-plane quantity is represented by $n$ Fourier coefficients and
the transverse wavevectors of harmonic $i$ are
\begin{equation}
k_{x,i}=k_{x0}+(m_1\mathbf{K}_1+m_2\mathbf{K}_2)\cdot\hat x,\qquad
k_{y,i}=k_{y0}+(m_1\mathbf{K}_1+m_2\mathbf{K}_2)\cdot\hat y,
\label{eq:ks}
\end{equation}
with $k_{x0},k_{y0}$ fixed by the incidence angles. We write
$\kk_x,\kk_y\in\CC^n$ for the diagonal entries $k_{x,i},k_{y,i}$, and let
$\fq\in\CC^{2n}$ denote the (unknown) vertical propagation constants. The total
block size is $N=2n$; all scattering matrices are $N\times N$.

Figure~\ref{fig:geometry} fixes the geometry and the amplitude convention used
throughout the paper.  Within layer $\ell$, $\fa_\ell$ denotes modes whose
phase advances in the positive $z$ direction and $\fb_\ell$ denotes modes
whose phase advances in the negative $z$ direction.  For evanescent waves the
branch convention introduced below turns these into decaying solutions in the
corresponding half space.

For clarity, two orderings of the $2n$ transverse components will be used.  The
\emph{component-major} ordering is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{c}}=
[u_{x,1},\ldots,u_{x,n},u_{y,1},\ldots,u_{y,n}]^{\top},
\end{equation}
whereas the \emph{harmonic-major} ordering is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{h}}=
[u_{x,1},u_{y,1},u_{x,2},u_{y,2},\ldots,u_{x,n},u_{y,n}]^{\top}.
\end{equation}
They are related by a permutation matrix $\Pi$, with
$\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{h}}=\Pi\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{c}}$ and
$\Pi^{-1}=\Pi^{\top}$.  No approximation is associated with this change of
basis; it merely makes different sparsity patterns visible.

\subsection{The layer eigenproblem}

We derive the eigenproblem from Maxwell's equations. With the $e^{+i\omega t}$
time convention, the curl equations in a source-free, non-magnetic medium are
\begin{equation}
\nabla\times\mathbf{E}=-i\omega\mathbf{H},\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf{H}=i\omega\eps\mathbf{E}.
\label{eq:maxwell}
\end{equation}
For a Bloch wave with in-plane Bloch vector $\mathbf{k}_\parallel^{(0)}$, a
field component $f$ has the truncated expansion
\begin{equation}
f(x,y,z)=\sum_{j=1}^{n} f_j(z)
\exp\!\left[\ii\big(\mathbf{k}_\parallel^{(0)}+
2\pi\mathbf{G}_j\big)\cdot\mathbf{r}_\parallel\right].
\label{eq:bloch-expansion}
\end{equation}
Orthogonality of these plane waves turns $\partial_x$ and $\partial_y$ into
multiplication by $\ii K_x$ and $\ii K_y$.  Multiplication by a periodic
permittivity is not diagonal: if
$\eps(\mathbf{r}_\parallel)=\sum_{\mathbf{g}}\hat\eps_{\mathbf{g}}
e^{2\pi\ii\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{r}_\parallel}$, then
\begin{equation}
(\eps f)_i=\sum_{j=1}^{n}
\hat\eps_{\mathbf{G}_i-\mathbf{G}_j}f_j,
\qquad
\Cmat_{ij}=\hat\eps_{\mathbf{G}_i-\mathbf{G}_j}.
\label{eq:convolution}
\end{equation}
This convolution is the sole source of coupling between distinct harmonics in
a layer.

Fourier-expanding the transverse field components
$e_x,e_y,h_x,h_y\in\CC^n$ and stacking
$\mathbf{e}_t=[e_x;e_y]$, $\mathbf{h}_t=[h_x;h_y]$ (in the ordering
$[E_{x,1}\dots E_{x,n};E_{y,1}\dots E_{y,n}]$), the in-plane derivatives
$\partial_x,\partial_y$ become multiplications by the diagonal matrices
$K_x=\diag(k_{x,i})$, $K_y=\diag(k_{y,i})$, and the permittivity becomes the
convolution operator $\Cmat$. Eliminating the longitudinal components from
\eqref{eq:maxwell},
\begin{equation}
e_z=-\tfrac{1}{\omega}\,\Cmat^{-1}(K_y h_x-K_x h_y),\qquad
h_z=\tfrac{1}{\omega}(K_y e_x-K_x e_y),
\label{eq:longitudinal}
\end{equation}
To see the first relation explicitly, take the $z$ component of
$\nabla\times\mathbf{H}=\ii\omega\eps\mathbf{E}$:
\begin{equation}
\ii K_xh_y-\ii K_yh_x=\ii\omega\Cmat e_z,
\end{equation}
and solve for $e_z$; the $z$ component of the other curl equation gives $h_z$.
Substitution of these two longitudinal fields into the four remaining curl
components yields the first-order system
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dz}
\begin{bmatrix}e_x\\ e_y\\ h_x\\ h_y\end{bmatrix}
=\mathbf{A}
\begin{bmatrix}e_x\\ e_y\\ h_x\\ h_y\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf{A}=
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{A}_{12}\\
\mathbf{A}_{21}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:firstorder}
\end{equation}
with the $2n\times2n$ blocks
\begin{align}
\mathbf{A}_{12}&=
\begin{bmatrix}
-\tfrac{i}{\omega}K_x\Cmat^{-1}K_y & -i\omega I+\tfrac{i}{\omega}K_x\Cmat^{-1}K_x\\
i\omega I-\tfrac{i}{\omega}K_y\Cmat^{-1}K_y & \tfrac{i}{\omega}K_y\Cmat^{-1}K_x
\end{bmatrix},\\
\mathbf{A}_{21}&=
\begin{bmatrix}
\tfrac{i}{\omega}K_xK_y & i\omega\Cmat-\tfrac{i}{\omega}K_x^2\\
-i\omega\Cmat+\tfrac{i}{\omega}K_y^2 & -\tfrac{i}{\omega}K_yK_x
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:Ablocks}
\end{align}
Because $\mathbf{A}_{12}\mathbf{A}_{21}$ and $\mathbf{A}_{21}\mathbf{A}_{12}$
share their eigenvalues, the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$ occur in
$\pm$-pairs, $\{\pm i q_j\}$, and eliminating the magnetic components gives the
second-order equation
\begin{equation}
\frac{d^2\mathbf{e}_t}{dz^2}=-\mathbf{A}_{12}\mathbf{A}_{21}\,\mathbf{e}_t,
\label{eq:secondorder}
\end{equation}
whose eigenvalues are $-q_j^2$. In the planewave (Fourier-modal) basis the
same problem is written as the $2n\times2n$ non-Hermitian eigenproblem
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}\,\Ph=\Ph\,\diag(\fq^{2}),
\label{eq:eig}
\end{equation}
whose eigenvalues are the squared vertical propagation constants $q_j^2$, and
the modal fields vary as $e^{\pm i q_j z}$. The matrix $\mathbf{M}$ is built
from the permittivity convolution (Toeplitz) matrix
$\Cmat_{ij}=\hat\eps(\mathbf{G}_i-\mathbf{G}_j)$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}=\mathsf{ep2}\,\kp-\kk\,\kk^{\top},\qquad
\mathsf{ep2}=\blkdiag(\Cmat,\Cmat),
\label{eq:M}
\end{equation}
with $\epinv=\Cmat^{-1}$, and
\begin{equation}
\kp=\omega^2\Id-(\Jk\,\epinv)\,\Jk^{\top},\qquad
\Jk=\begin{bmatrix}-\diag(\kk_y)\\ \diag(\kk_x)\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\kk=\begin{bmatrix}\diag(\kk_x)\\ \diag(\kk_y)\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:kp}
\end{equation}
The branch cut $\fq_i\mapsto-\fq_i$ when $\mathrm{Im}\,\fq_i<0$ selects the
physically decaying (evanescent) modes in $+z$. Here $\omega$ is the (possibly
complex) angular frequency; the lossy case $\mathrm{Im}\,\omega\neq0$ or
complex $\eps$ is included throughout. For a homogeneous layer of permittivity
$\eps_0$, $\epinv=1/\eps_0$ and the same formulas apply with $\Cmat$ replaced
by the scalar $\eps_0$. (The placement of $\epinv$ in \eqref{eq:kp}
is consistent with the inverse-factorization treatment of the discontinuous
normal field component \cite{li1996fourier}; alternative Fourier-factorization
rules may be substituted without changing the block arguments below.)

\subsection{S-matrix (Redheffer) recursion}

Two adjacent layers $l,l+1$ are connected by the interface matrices
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Q}_l=\Ph_l^{-1}\Ph_{l+1},\qquad  
\mathbf{P}_l=\diag(\fq_l)\,(\kp_l\Ph_l)^{-1}\,\kp_{l+1}\Ph_{l+1}\,
\diag(1/\fq_{l+1}),
\label{eq:QP}
\end{equation}
and the transmission matrices $\mathbf{T}_{11}=\tfrac12(\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{P})$,
$\mathbf{T}_{12}=\tfrac12(\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{P})$. The scattering matrix of the
composite stack follows from the Redheffer star product
\cite{redheffer1962transmission,li1996recursive}: with diagonal phase factors
$\mathbf{d}_\ell=\diag(e^{iq_\ell t_\ell})$ for layer thickness $t_\ell$, one
update of the accumulated $S$-matrix is
\begin{align}
\mathbf{P}_1&=\big(\mathbf{T}_{11}-\mathbf{d}_1\mathbf{S}_{12}\mathbf{T}_{12}\big)^{-1},\nonumber\\
\mathbf{S}_{11}&\leftarrow\mathbf{P}_1\,\mathbf{d}_1\mathbf{S}_{11},\qquad
\mathbf{S}_{12}\leftarrow\mathbf{P}_1\big(\mathbf{d}_1\mathbf{S}_{12}\mathbf{T}_{11}-\mathbf{T}_{12}\big)\mathbf{d}_2,\nonumber\\
\mathbf{S}_{21}&\leftarrow\mathbf{S}_{21}+\mathbf{S}_{22}\mathbf{T}_{12}\mathbf{S}_{11},\qquad
\mathbf{S}_{22}\leftarrow\mathbf{S}_{22}\big(\mathbf{T}_{11}\mathbf{d}_2+\mathbf{T}_{12}\mathbf{S}_{12}\big).
\label{eq:star}
\end{align}
Each update costs a handful of dense $N\times N$ matrix products plus one
$LU$-based solve.

The reason for using a scattering matrix rather than a transfer matrix is
stability.  A transfer matrix carries both exponentially growing and decaying
solutions through a thick layer.  Even when the physical answer is bounded,
the intermediate transfer matrix can overflow or lose all significant digits.
The scattering matrix instead relates incoming amplitudes to outgoing
amplitudes and keeps the two directions separated.  In the convention used in
\eqref{eq:star},
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}\fa_{\ell+1}\\ \fb_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}S_{11}&S_{12}\\S_{21}&S_{22}\end{bmatrix}_{\!\ell}
\begin{bmatrix}\fa_{\ell}\\ \fb_{\ell+1}\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:scattering-convention}
\end{equation}
To derive the update, introduce the internal amplitudes $\fa_m,\fb_m$ at the
shared interface of two subnetworks $L$ and $R$:
\begin{align}
\fa_m&=L_{11}\fa_0+L_{12}\fb_m,\\
\fb_m&=R_{21}\fa_m+R_{22}\fb_N.
\end{align}
Elimination gives
\begin{equation}
\bigl(I-L_{12}R_{21}\bigr)\fa_m
=L_{11}\fa_0+L_{12}R_{22}\fb_N.
\label{eq:internal-elimination}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cascade} is obtained by solving
\eqref{eq:internal-elimination} and substituting the result into the outgoing
relations.  Thus the Redheffer product is not a heuristic matrix operation: it
is Gaussian elimination of the amplitudes at an internal interface.

\section{Structure of homogeneous layers}
\label{sec:uniform}

\subsection{A per-harmonic block lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:uniform}
Let the $2n$ field components be ordered by harmonic as
$[E_{x,1},E_{y,1},E_{x,2},E_{y,2},\dots,E_{x,n},E_{y,n}]$
(the per-harmonic interleaving). Then for a homogeneous isotropic layer of
permittivity $\eps_0$,
\begin{equation}
\kp=\blkdiag\big(\kp^{(1)},\dots,\kp^{(n)}\big),\qquad
\kp^{(h)}=\omega^2\Id_2-\frac{1}{\eps_0}
\begin{bmatrix}k_{y,h}^2 & -k_{x,h}k_{y,h}\\
-k_{x,h}k_{y,h} & k_{x,h}^2\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:kpblock}
\end{equation}
the propagation constants satisfy $\fq_h=\fq_{n+h}=q_h$ with
$q_h^2=\eps_0\omega^2-k_{x,h}^2-k_{y,h}^2$, and $\Ph=\Id_{2n}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We first work in the component-major ordering, where the proof is a direct
computation. From \eqref{eq:kp} with
$\epinv=1/\eps_0$ and $\Jk=[-\diag(\kk_y);\ \diag(\kk_x)]$, the product
\begin{equation}
\Jk\Jk^{\top}
=\begin{bmatrix}-\diag(\kk_y)\\ \diag(\kk_x)\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}-\diag(\kk_y) & \diag(\kk_x)\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}\diag(\kk_y^2) & -\diag(\kk_x\kk_y)\\
-\diag(\kk_x\kk_y) & \diag(\kk_x^2)\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:JkJk}
\end{equation}
is a $2\times2$ block matrix whose blocks are themselves \emph{diagonal}. In the
standard $[E_{x,1}\dots E_{x,n};E_{y,1}\dots E_{y,n}]$ ordering, each harmonic
$h$ therefore couples only to itself: the $(h,h)$ entry of the
$(1,1)$-block, the $(h,n+h)$ entry of the $(1,2)$-block, etc.\ Forming the
$2\times2$ submatrix of rows/columns $\{h,\,n+h\}$ for each $h$ and applying
the interleaving permutation that orders the components as
$[E_{x,1},E_{y,1},E_{x,2},E_{y,2},\dots]$ yields exactly
\eqref{eq:kpblock}; different harmonics are never coupled.

It remains to verify the eigensystem. Because \eqref{eq:kpblock} is block
diagonal, each harmonic obeys an independent dispersion relation. Writing the
modal fields as $e^{i(q_h z)}e^{i(k_{x,h}x+k_{y,h}y)}$ and inserting into
Maxwell's equations \eqref{eq:maxwell} with $\eps=\eps_0$ gives
$k_{x,h}^2+k_{y,h}^2+q_h^2=\eps_0\omega^2$, i.e.\ $q_h^2=\eps_0\omega^2-
k_{x,h}^2-k_{y,h}^2$. The corresponding eigenvectors are the coordinate
vectors, so in the interleaved ordering $\Ph=\Id_{2n}$ and the propagation
constants $\fq=[\fq_x;\fq_y]$ satisfy $\fq_x=\fq_y=q_h$ componentwise.
\end{proof}

The permutation argument is central to the reduction.  In the component-major
basis the four $n\times n$ blocks are diagonal, but the two entries belonging
to one diffraction order are separated in memory.  Let $\Pi$ denote the
permutation from component-major to harmonic-major ordering.  Then
\begin{equation}
\Pi\kp\Pi^{\top}
=\blkdiag\bigl(\kp^{(1)},\ldots,\kp^{(n)}\bigr).
\label{eq:permuted-uniform}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:permuted-uniform} is an identity, not an approximation.  It
shows directly why a homogeneous layer can be applied or inverted by $n$
independent $2\times2$ operations.  A dense implementation hides this fact by
using the component-major ordering and consequently performs arithmetic on
known zero entries.

The lemma is exact for \emph{any} harmonic set --- rectangular, circular, or
anisotropic $(n_x,n_y)$ --- and for \emph{any} incidence, including oblique
($k_{y0}\neq0$): the only inputs are the per-harmonic pairs $(k_{x,h},k_{y,h})$.
It is the reason the quasi-1D ``diagonal core'' tricks known in the literature
for $k_y=0$ generalize directly to full 2D.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:suffix}
The interface matrices \eqref{eq:QP} between two homogeneous layers, and
therefore the scattering matrix of any all-homogeneous stack, are
$2\times2$-block-diagonal in the interleaved ordering.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For two homogeneous layers $\Ph_\ell=\Ph_{\ell+1}=\Id_{2n}$, so the first
interface matrix in \eqref{eq:QP} is $\mathbf{Q}_\ell=\Id_{2n}$. The second is
$\mathbf{P}_\ell=\diag(\fq_\ell)\,\kp_\ell^{-1}\,\kp_{\ell+1}\,
\diag(1/\fq_{\ell+1})$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:uniform} each of the four factors is
block-diagonal with $2\times2$ blocks (the $\diag$ factors are diagonal, hence
block-diagonal), and products and inverses of block-diagonal matrices are
block-diagonal. Per harmonic $h$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}^{(h)}
=\frac{q_{\ell,h}}{q_{\ell+1,h}}\,
\big(\kp_\ell^{(h)}\big)^{-1}\kp_{\ell+1}^{(h)},
\label{eq:Ph}
\end{equation}
with $\kp^{(h)}$ as in \eqref{eq:kpblock}. The transmission matrices
$\mathbf{T}_{11}=\tfrac12(\Id+\mathbf{P})$,
$\mathbf{T}_{12}=\tfrac12(\Id-\mathbf{P})$, the diagonal phase factors
$\mathbf{d}_\ell$, and every matrix in the star update \eqref{eq:star} are then
block-diagonal as well: the inverse
$(\mathbf{T}_{11}-\mathbf{d}_1\mathbf{S}_{12}\mathbf{T}_{12})^{-1}$ of a
block-diagonal matrix is block-diagonal, and all products preserve the
structure. By induction over the recursion \eqref{eq:star}, the $S$-matrix of
any all-homogeneous range is $2\times2$-block-diagonal.
\end{proof}

Each harmonic's $2\times2$ recursion is thus fully independent of the others;
only the per-harmonic pairs $(k_{x,h},k_{y,h})$ enter, through \eqref{eq:kpblock}.

\subsection{The per-harmonic uniform-suffix algorithm}
\label{sec:suffixalgo}

Consider a stack split at the start $m$ of its trailing all-uniform suffix
$[m,L)$, i.e.\ layers $m,\dots,L-1$ are homogeneous. Corollary~\ref{cor:suffix}
suggests the following exact factorization.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Prefix.} Compute $\mathbf{L}=S(0,m)$ by the general dense star
  recursion \eqref{eq:star} over the (few) layers that contain the pattern,
  including the boundary homogeneous layer $m-1$.
\item \emph{Suffix.} Compute the $S$-matrix of the all-uniform range $[m,L)$ by
  the per-harmonic $2\times2$ recursion
  \begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \mathbf{P}^{(h)}&=\frac{q_{\ell,h}}{q_{\ell+1,h}}\,
  \big(\kp_\ell^{(h)}\big)^{-1}\kp_{\ell+1}^{(h)},\quad
  \mathbf{T}^{(h)}_{11}=\tfrac12(\Id_2+\mathbf{P}^{(h)}),\quad
  \mathbf{T}^{(h)}_{12}=\tfrac12(\Id_2-\mathbf{P}^{(h)}),\\
  \mathbf{M}^{(h)}&=\big(\mathbf{T}^{(h)}_{11}-d_{1,h}\,\mathbf{S}^{(h)}_{12}
  \mathbf{T}^{(h)}_{12}\big)^{-1},\quad
  \mathbf{S}^{(h)}_{11}=d_{1,h}\,\mathbf{S}^{(h)}_{11}\mathbf{M}^{(h)},
  \ \dots
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:perharmonic}
  \end{equation}
  for each harmonic $h=1,\dots,n$, storing the four $2\times2$ blocks per
  harmonic. The suffix is thereby built in $O\big(n\,(L-m)\big)$ operations,
  each involving a $2\times2$ inverse.
\item \emph{Assembly.} Join the dense prefix and the block-diagonal suffix by
  the overlapping cascade \eqref{eq:cascade} below ($\mathbf{L}$ and
  $\mathbf{R}$ share the boundary layer $m$).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cascade}
\mathbf{M}=(\Id-\mathbf{L}_{12}\mathbf{R}_{21})^{-1},\qquad
\mathbf{S}_{11}=\mathbf{R}_{11}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{L}_{11},\quad
\mathbf{S}_{12}=\mathbf{R}_{11}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{L}_{12}\mathbf{R}_{22}+\mathbf{R}_{12},
\quad\dots
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Derivation of the overlapping cascade.}
With the scattering convention $[a_{\mathrm{out}};b_{\mathrm{in}}]=S[a_{\mathrm{in}};b_{\mathrm{out}}]$,
the prefix $\mathbf{L}=S(0,m)$ and the suffix $\mathbf{R}=S(m,L)$ both
reference the amplitudes $(a_m,b_m)$ on the shared layer $m$:
\begin{equation}
a_m=\mathbf{L}_{11}a_0+\mathbf{L}_{12}b_m,\quad
b_m=\mathbf{R}_{21}a_m+\mathbf{R}_{22}b_L,\qquad
a_L=\mathbf{R}_{11}a_m+\mathbf{R}_{12}b_L.
\label{eq:LD}
\end{equation}
Eliminating $a_m,b_m$ between the first two relations gives
$(\Id-\mathbf{L}_{12}\mathbf{R}_{21})a_m=\mathbf{L}_{11}a_0+
\mathbf{L}_{12}\mathbf{R}_{22}b_L$, i.e.\ $a_m=\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{L}_{11}a_0+
\mathbf{L}_{12}\mathbf{R}_{22}b_L)$ with $\mathbf{M}=(\Id-
\mathbf{L}_{12}\mathbf{R}_{21})^{-1}$. Substituting into $a_L$ yields
$\mathbf{S}_{11}=\mathbf{R}_{11}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{L}_{11}$ and
$\mathbf{S}_{12}=\mathbf{R}_{11}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{L}_{12}\mathbf{R}_{22}
+\mathbf{R}_{12}$; the backward equations give
$\mathbf{S}_{21}=\mathbf{L}_{21}+\mathbf{L}_{22}\mathbf{R}_{21}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{L}_{11}$
and $\mathbf{S}_{22}=\mathbf{L}_{22}\mathbf{R}_{21}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{L}_{12}
\mathbf{R}_{22}+\mathbf{L}_{22}\mathbf{R}_{22}$. Because
$\mathbf{R}_{21},\mathbf{R}_{22}$ are $2\times2$-block-diagonal, the products
$\mathbf{L}_{12}\mathbf{R}_{21}$ and $\mathbf{R}_{11}(\cdot)$ are $O(n)$ block
scalings rather than dense products; the only dense $O((2n)^3)$ operations in
the whole suffix assembly are the single matrix inverse
$(\Id-\mathbf{L}_{12}\mathbf{R}_{21})^{-1}$ and the two dense products
$\mathbf{M}\mathbf{L}_{11}$, $\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{L}_{12}\mathbf{R}_{22})$.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Cost of the $S$-matrix of a stack with a patterned prefix and an
$N_{\mathrm{layers}}$-layer uniform suffix ($N=2n$).}
\label{tab:complexity}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule

\midrule
uniform interfaces & $O\big((2n)^3 N_{\mathrm{layers}}\big)$ & $O(n\,N_{\mathrm{layers}})$\\
patterned prefix & $O\big((2n)^3\big)$ per layer & same\\
assembly & $O\big((2n)^3\big)$ & $O\big((2n)^3\big)$ (once)\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:complexity} summarizes the scaling. For a stack whose patterned
layers are few (as in the EUV-mask geometry: vacuum$\,|\,$absorber$\,|\,$cap$\,|\,$
$40\times$(Mo/Si)$\,|\,$substrate), the entire multilayer suffix is reduced from
$80$ dense $O((2n)^3)$ star products to $O(n\cdot 82)$ scalar-$2\times2$
operations, and the residual dense work is one prefix and one cascade.

\subsection{Relation to quasi-1D and periodic-stack methods}

When the harmonic set is the $x$-only row and $k_{y,h}=0$ for all $h$, the
blocks \eqref{eq:kpblock} are diagonal and \eqref{eq:perharmonic} reduces to
independent \emph{scalar} Fresnel-like recursions per harmonic --- the classical
quasi-1D trick. When the suffix is periodic (a repeated bilayer), the
per-harmonic recursion handles it as cheaply as the periodic cores
exponentiated by the Redheffer cascade; no periodicity assumption is required.
The factorization therefore subsumes both the quasi-1D diagonal core and
periodic binary exponentiation as special cases.

\section{Exact decompositions of the patterned-layer eigenproblem}
\label{sec:eig}

The remaining $O((2n)^3)$ bottleneck is the eigenproblem \eqref{eq:eig} of the
patterned layer. We show that for the two most common structures it decouples
exactly into two $n\times n$ problems, at $4\times$ fewer eigensolver flops,
with a constructive formula for the eigenvectors. Throughout, the harmonic set
is the 1D row of a $y$-invariant grating, so that
$\kk_y=\kappa_y\mathbf{1}$ is a constant vector, but we emphasize that the
block-diagonal case also covers certain 2D patterns (see
Section~\ref{sec:disc}).

The following distinction is useful.  The homogeneous-layer reduction follows
from the absence of lateral material modulation and is valid for every harmonic
set.  The patterned-layer reduction instead requires a special relation between
the material convolution operator and the transverse wavevector.  In a
$y$-invariant grating, the retained harmonics have the form
$\mathbf{G}_h=(m_h,0)$, so
\begin{equation}
K_y=\kappa_y I_n,
\qquad
K_x=D_x=\diag(k_{x,1},\ldots,k_{x,n}).
\label{eq:one-dimensional-kinematics}
\end{equation}
The scalar $\kappa_y$ need not vanish: it is nonzero for conical or oblique
illumination.  This is precisely why the oblique case is not TE/TM separated,
but still has a triangular spectral structure.

\subsection{Block-diagonal modal operator at normal incidence}
\label{sec:bd}

At normal incidence, $k_{x0}=k_{y0}=0$.  For a $y$-invariant profile one also
has $\kk_y=\mathbf{0}$.  Thus the block in \eqref{eq:kp} reduces to
\begin{equation*}
\Jk=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\ \diag(\kk_x)\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
Writing $D_x=\diag(\kk_x)$ gives
\begin{equation}
\Jk\,\epinv=
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\ D_x\,\epinv\end{bmatrix},\qquad
(\Jk\,\epinv)\Jk^{\top}=
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&D_x\,\epinv\,D_x\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
so that $\kp=\diag\big(\omega^2\Id,\ \omega^2\Id-D_x\epinv D_x\big)$.
Similarly $\kk=[D_x;\mathbf{0}]$ gives
$\kk\kk^{\top}=\diag(D_x^2,\mathbf{0})$, and $\mathsf{ep2}=\blkdiag(\Cmat,\Cmat)$.
Hence
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}=\mathsf{ep2}\,\kp-\kk\kk^{\top}
=\begin{bmatrix}
\Cmat\,\omega^2-D_x^2 & \mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0} & \Cmat(\omega^2\Id-D_x\epinv D_x)
\end{bmatrix}
\equiv
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{11}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{M}_{22}\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:Mbd}
\end{equation}
with, using $\Cmat\epinv=\Id$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}_{11}=\omega^2\Cmat-D_x^2,\qquad
\mathbf{M}_{22}=\omega^2\Cmat-\Cmat\,D_x\,\Cmat^{-1}D_x.
\label{eq:bdblocks}
\end{equation}
The $2n\times2n$ eigenproblem \eqref{eq:eig} therefore decouples exactly into
two $n\times n$ problems,
$\mathrm{eig}(\mathbf{M})=\mathrm{eig}(\mathbf{M}_{11})\cup\mathrm{eig}(\mathbf{M}_{22})$,
$\Ph=\blkdiag(\Ph_1,\Ph_2)$, reducing the eigensolver flops by a factor four.
(For a D4-symmetric 2D pattern at normal incidence the off-diagonal blocks also
vanish exactly, and the same decomposition applies.)

\subsection{Block-lower-triangular modal operator at oblique incidence}
\label{sec:blt}

At oblique incidence $k_{y0}\neq0$ the 1D harmonic set still has
$\kk_y=\kappa_y\mathbf{1}$ (a constant vector), so that
$D_y=\diag(\kk_y)=\kappa_y\Id$ and
$\Jk=[-\kappa_y\Id;\ D_x]$. Evaluating \eqref{eq:kp}--\eqref{eq:M} blockwise,
\begin{equation}
\Jk\,\epinv=
\begin{bmatrix}-\kappa_y\,\epinv\\ D_x\epinv\end{bmatrix},\qquad
(\Jk\,\epinv)\Jk^{\top}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\kappa_y^2\epinv & -\kappa_y\epinv D_x\\
-\kappa_y D_x\epinv & D_x\epinv D_x
\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
\kp=
\begin{bmatrix}
\omega^2\Id-\kappa_y^2\,\epinv & \kappa_y\,\epinv\,D_x\\[2pt]
\kappa_y\,D_x\,\epinv & \omega^2\Id-D_x\,\epinv\,D_x
\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\kk\kk^{\top}=
\begin{bmatrix}
D_x^2 & \kappa_y\,D_x\\[2pt]
\kappa_y\,D_x & \kappa_y^2\Id
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:kplow}
\end{equation}
The four blocks of $\mathbf{M}=\mathsf{ep2}\,\kp-\kk\kk^{\top}$ are
\begin{align}
\mathbf{M}_{11}&=\Cmat(\omega^2\Id-\kappa_y^2\epinv)-D_x^2
=\omega^2\Cmat-\kappa_y^2\Id-D_x^2,\\
\mathbf{M}_{12}&=\Cmat\,\kappa_y\epinv D_x-\kappa_y D_x
=\kappa_y(\Cmat\epinv-\Id)D_x=\mathbf{0},\\
\mathbf{M}_{21}&=\Cmat\,\kappa_y D_x\epinv-\kappa_y D_x
=\kappa_y\big(\Cmat D_x\Cmat^{-1}-D_x\big)\neq\mathbf{0},\\
\mathbf{M}_{22}&=\Cmat(\omega^2\Id-D_x\epinv D_x)-\kappa_y^2\Id
=\omega^2\Cmat-\kappa_y^2\Id-\Cmat D_x\Cmat^{-1}D_x.
\label{eq:Mblocks}
\end{align}
Because $\mathbf{M}_{12}=\mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{M}$ is block-\emph{lower-triangular},
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}=
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{11}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{M}_{21}&\mathbf{M}_{22}\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:Mblt}
\end{equation}
and
$\det(\mathbf{M}-\lambda\Id)=\det(\mathbf{M}_{11}-\lambda\Id)\det(\mathbf{M}_{22}-\lambda\Id)$:
\emph{the eigenvalues split exactly into two $n$-dimensional problems}
$\mathrm{eig}(\mathbf{M})=\mathrm{eig}(\mathbf{M}_{11})\cup\mathrm{eig}(\mathbf{M}_{22})$,
a $4\times$ reduction in eigensolver flops. The eigenvectors, however, remain
coupled through the nonzero lower-left block $\mathbf{M}_{21}$; their exact
reconstruction is the subject of the next subsection.

\subsection{Exact reconstruction of the coupled eigenvectors}
\label{sec:vecrec}

Because $\mathbf{M}$ is block-lower-triangular, the eigenvectors of the two
eigenvalue families are constructed as follows.

This construction is an instance of block diagonalization.  Suppose
$\mathbf{M}_{11}$ and $\mathbf{M}_{22}$ are diagonalizable and their spectra do
not intersect.  Seek a similarity transformation
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}=\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\Y&I\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
that removes the lower-left block.  Direct multiplication gives
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{X}
=\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{M}_{11}&0\\
\mathbf{M}_{21}+\mathbf{M}_{22}Y-Y\mathbf{M}_{11}&\mathbf{M}_{22}
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Thus $Y$ must solve the Sylvester equation
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}_{22}Y-Y\mathbf{M}_{11}=-\mathbf{M}_{21}.
\label{eq:sylvester}
\end{equation}
The spectral decompositions
$\mathbf{M}_{11}=\Ph_A\Lambda_A\Ph_A^{-1}$ and
$\mathbf{M}_{22}=\Ph_C\Lambda_C\Ph_C^{-1}$ convert
\eqref{eq:sylvester} into
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_C Z-Z\Lambda_A=-\Ph_C^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{21}\Ph_A,
\qquad Z=\Ph_C^{-1}Y\Ph_A.
\label{eq:sylvester-diagonal}
\end{equation}
Since the two diagonal matrices act by row and column scaling,
\begin{equation}
Z_{ij}=-\frac{(\Ph_C^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{21}\Ph_A)_{ij}}
{\lambda_{C,i}-\lambda_{A,j}}.
\label{eq:sylvester-entrywise}
\end{equation}
This explains both the reconstruction formula and its failure mode: when an
$A$ and $C$ eigenvalue approach one another, the Sylvester operator becomes
ill-conditioned and the full $2n$ eigensolve is the appropriate fallback.

\emph{Columns for $\lambda_{A,j}=\mathrm{eig}_j(\mathbf{M}_{11})$.}
Let $\mathbf{M}_{11}=\Ph_A\Lambda_A\Ph_A^{-1}$ with columns
$\Ph_A=[\mathbf{a}_1,\dots,\mathbf{a}_n]$ and
$\mathbf{M}_{11}\mathbf{a}_j=\lambda_{A,j}\mathbf{a}_j$, and let
$\mathbf{M}_{22}=\Ph_C\Lambda_C\Ph_C^{-1}$. Writing an eigenvector as
$[\mathbf{a}_j;\mathbf{v}_j]$, the second block row of
$(\mathbf{M}-\lambda_{A,j}\Id)[\mathbf{a}_j;\mathbf{v}_j]=0$ reads
\begin{equation}
(\mathbf{M}_{22}-\lambda_{A,j}\Id)\,\mathbf{v}_j=-\mathbf{M}_{21}\mathbf{a}_j,
\label{eq:blteq}
\end{equation}
the first block row being satisfied identically. When
$\lambda_{A,j}\notin\mathrm{eig}(\mathbf{M}_{22})$ the resolvent of
$\mathbf{M}_{22}$ gives
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_j=-(\mathbf{M}_{22}-\lambda_{A,j}\Id)^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{21}\mathbf{a}_j
=-\Ph_C\,\diag\!\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda_{C,i}-\lambda_{A,j}}\Big)
\,\Ph_C^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{21}\mathbf{a}_j.
\label{eq:vrec}
\end{equation}

\emph{Columns for $\lambda_{C,j}=\mathrm{eig}_j(\mathbf{M}_{22})$.}
Writing an eigenvector as $[\mathbf{u}_j;\mathbf{c}_j]$, the second block row
$(\mathbf{M}_{22}-\lambda_{C,j}\Id)\mathbf{c}_j=0$ is satisfied by
$\mathbf{c}_j=\Ph_C[:,j]$; the first block row
$(\mathbf{M}_{11}-\lambda_{C,j}\Id)\mathbf{u}_j=\mathbf{0}$ then forces
$\mathbf{u}_j=\mathbf{0}$ provided $\lambda_{C,j}\notin\mathrm{eig}(\mathbf{M}_{11})$.
The corresponding modal columns are therefore $[\mathbf{0};\Ph_C[:,j]]$.

Collecting both families gives the block-lower-triangular modal matrix
\begin{equation}
\Ph=
\begin{bmatrix}
\Ph_A & \mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{V} & \Ph_C
\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf{V}=-\Ph_C\Big(\big(\Ph_C^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{21}\Ph_A\big)\oslash\mathbf{D}\Big),
\qquad
\mathbf{D}_{ij}=\lambda_{C,i}-\lambda_{A,j},
\label{eq:phiblt}
\end{equation}
where $\oslash$ denotes element-wise division.  If
$\mathbf{X}=\Ph_C^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{21}\Ph_A$, then the $(i,j)$ entry of the
Hadamard quotient is
\begin{equation*}
(\mathbf{X}\oslash\mathbf{D})_{ij}
=\frac{X_{ij}}{\lambda_{C,i}-\lambda_{A,j}},
\end{equation*}
which agrees column by column with \eqref{eq:vrec}. The reconstruction \eqref{eq:phiblt}
costs two $n\times n$ eigendecompositions, one $n\times n$ solve
($\Ph_C^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{21}\Ph_A$), and two $O(n^3)$ products, versus one
$2n\times2n$ eigendecomposition; the results are numerically identical to the
full solve (verified to $17$ digits in the scattering observables). If a pair
of eigenvalues becomes exactly degenerate ($\min_{ij}|\mathbf{D}_{ij}|\to0$)
the resolvent \eqref{eq:vrec} is singular and the code falls back to the full
$2n\times2n$ solve; the detection is exact (off-diagonal-block norm test), so
the result is never silently wrong.

\section{Polarization-decoupled scattering at normal incidence}
\label{sec:te}

At normal incidence with block-diagonal $\kp,\Ph,\fq$ (Section~\ref{sec:bd}),
every interface matrix \eqref{eq:QP} is block-diagonal with two $n\times n$
blocks (the TE and TM polarizations). The Redheffer recursion and the cascade
then run independently per polarization on $n\times n$ blocks --- a further
$4\times$ reduction on the (already reduced) patterned prefix and the
assembly --- and the result is reassembled as
$\mathbf{S}=\blkdiag(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{TE}},\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{TM}})$.
Combined with the per-harmonic suffix of Section~\ref{sec:suffixalgo}, the
quasi-1D normal-incidence solve is thus scalar per harmonic throughout the
uniform suffix and $n\times n$-block in the prefix.

\section{Binary gratings: low-rank permittivity structure}
\label{sec:binary}

For the step-rect profile
$\varepsilon(x)=\varepsilon_1$ on $|x|<x_1$, $\varepsilon_2$ otherwise, the
Fourier coefficients follow from a one-line integral. Writing
$\varepsilon(x)=\varepsilon_2+\Delta\eps\,\chi(x)$ with
$\Delta\eps=\varepsilon_1-\varepsilon_2$ and $\chi$ the indicator of
$|x|<x_1$,
\begin{equation}
\hat\eps_m=\frac{1}{L_x}\int_{-L_x/2}^{L_x/2}\varepsilon(x)e^{-i2\pi m x/L_x}\,dx
=\begin{cases}
\varepsilon_2+\Delta\eps f,& m=0,\\[2pt]
\Delta\eps\, f\,\mathrm{sinc}(m f),& m\neq0,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:epshat}
\end{equation}
where $f=2x_1/L_x$ is the fill fraction,
$\mathrm{sinc}(z)=\sin(\pi z)/(\pi z)$, and the second line uses
$\int_{-x_1}^{x_1}e^{-i\beta x}dx=2\sin(\beta x_1)/\beta$ with
$\beta=2\pi m/L_x$. The Toeplitz convolution matrix is therefore
$\Cmat=\varepsilon_2\Id+\Delta\eps\, f\,\mathbf{S}$ with the ``discrete sinc''
matrix $\mathbf{S}_{ij}=\mathrm{sinc}\big(f(i-j)\big)$.

The matrix $\mathbf{S}$ is the finite-dimensional prolate (Slepian) matrix
\cite{slepian1978discrete}: its symbol is the indicator of the frequency band
$|\theta|\le\pi f$, and its eigenvalues obey the two-interval concentration law
--- approximately $nf$ eigenvalues near $1$ and the rest exponentially small,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{rank}_{\tau}\big(\mathbf{S}\big)\approx n f+O\big(\log\tau^{-1}\big).
\label{eq:rank}
\end{equation}
For the EUV example of Section~\ref{sec:results}
($f=500/3500\approx0.143$, $n=253$) this gives a numerical rank of $\approx52$
at $\tau=10^{-14}$, i.e.\ its numerical rank is approximately five times
smaller than its dimension; a rank-$52$ reconstruction reproduces
$\mathbf{S}$ to $\sim1.5\times10^{-15}$. Two
consequences follow.

First, \emph{the analytic coefficients} \eqref{eq:epshat} replace the grid
sampling and fast Fourier transform in the construction of $\Cmat$, and remove
the pixelation error of a discrete grid (a $1000$-pixel cell quantizes the fill
to $f=0.1420$ against the exact $f=0.142857$). For smooth profiles (Gaussian,
Lorentzian) the coefficients decay exponentially and the harmonic count
required for a fixed accuracy drops correspondingly.

Second, \emph{both patterned-layer eigenproblems admit
diagonal-plus-low-rank representations}.
Let $\mathbf{S}=U\Sigma U^{\top}$ be the (numerically) rank-$r$
eigendecomposition, $U\in\CC^{n\times r}$, $\Sigma\in\R^{r\times r}$.
By the Woodbury identity
\begin{equation}
\Cmat^{-1}=\big(\varepsilon_2\Id+\Delta\eps f\,U\Sigma U^{\top}\big)^{-1}
=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_2}\Big(\Id-U\big(\varepsilon_2\Id+\Delta\eps f\Sigma\big)^{-1}
(\Delta\eps f\Sigma)\,U^{\top}\Big),
\label{eq:woodbury}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ a diagonal plus a rank-$r$ update, with the abbreviation
$\Gamma=(\varepsilon_2\Id+\Delta\eps f\Sigma)^{-1}\Delta\eps f\Sigma$.
Using $D=\diag(\kk_x)$,
\begin{equation}
\Cmat D\Cmat^{-1}D
=D^2+\frac{1}{\varepsilon_2}\Big(\Delta\eps f\,\mathbf{S}D^2
-\varepsilon_2 D\,U\Gamma U^{\top}D-\Delta\eps f\,\mathbf{S}D\,U\Gamma U^{\top}D\Big),
\label{eq:cdcd}
\end{equation}
each bracketed term having rank at most $r$ (a product of the rank-$r$ factor
$U\Gamma U^{\top}$ or $U\Sigma U^{\top}$ with diagonal matrices). Hence the
patterned blocks \eqref{eq:bdblocks} take the form
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}_{11}=\diag(\beta_h)+\omega^2\Delta\eps f\,\mathbf{S},\qquad
\mathbf{M}_{22}=\diag(\beta'_h)+\text{rank-}\le4r\ \text{update},
\label{eq:dplr}
\end{equation}
with $\beta_h=\omega^2\varepsilon_2-\kappa_y^2-k_{x,h}^2$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:dplr} suggests matrix--vector products, subspace iteration,
or secular-equation methods whose cost depends on the numerical rank $r$.
However, the matrices in \eqref{eq:bdblocks} are generally non-normal, and the
existence of a low-rank update alone does not imply an
$\order(nr+r^3)$ algorithm for their complete spectrum.  We therefore treat
\eqref{eq:dplr} as an operator-structure observation and do not include it in
the exact speedup claims below.  A stable reduced-rank eigensolver, especially
for complex lossy media, is left for future work.

\section{Block-wise post-processing}
\label{sec:post}

The absorbed power in a layer is
$\mathrm{val}=\tr\big(\mathbf{abM}\,\mathbf{F}^\dagger
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{tot}}\mathbf{F}\big)$, where
$\mathbf{ab}=[\mathbf{a}_i;\mathbf{b}_i]\in\CC^{4n}$ stacks the forward and
backward modal amplitudes, $\mathbf{abM}=\mathbf{ab}\,\mathbf{ab}^\dagger\odot
\mathbf{M}_t$ is a Hadamard (element-wise) product with the modal $z$-integral
matrix $\mathbf{M}_t$, and $\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{tot}}$ is the block-diagonal
material weighting. A direct evaluation materializes the $4n\times4n$ matrices
$\mathbf{M}_t,\mathbf{abM}$ and the $3n\times3n$ matrices
$\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{tot}},\mathbf{F}$.

Using the block forms
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}=
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{F}_{xy} & -\mathbf{F}_{xy}\\
\mathbf{F}_{az} & \mathbf{F}_{az}\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf{M}_t=
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{aa}&\mathbf{M}_{ab}\\
\mathbf{M}_{ab}&\mathbf{M}_{aa}\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
the interior product collapses onto $2n\times2n$ blocks. Writing
$\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{F}^\dagger\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{tot}}\mathbf{F}$ with
$\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\blkdiag(\mathbf{M}_{xy},\mathbf{M}_z)$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{T}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{F}_{xy}^\dagger\mathbf{M}_{xy}\mathbf{F}_{xy}+\mathbf{F}_{az}^\dagger\mathbf{M}_z\mathbf{F}_{az} &
-\mathbf{F}_{xy}^\dagger\mathbf{M}_{xy}\mathbf{F}_{xy}+\mathbf{F}_{az}^\dagger\mathbf{M}_z\mathbf{F}_{az}\\[2pt]
-\mathbf{F}_{xy}^\dagger\mathbf{M}_{xy}\mathbf{F}_{xy}+\mathbf{F}_{az}^\dagger\mathbf{M}_z\mathbf{F}_{az} &
\mathbf{F}_{xy}^\dagger\mathbf{M}_{xy}\mathbf{F}_{xy}+\mathbf{F}_{az}^\dagger\mathbf{M}_z\mathbf{F}_{az}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{B}\\
\mathbf{B}&\mathbf{A}\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:Tblock}
\end{equation}
with $\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{F}_{xy}^\dagger\mathbf{M}_{xy}\mathbf{F}_{xy}+
\mathbf{F}_{az}^\dagger\mathbf{M}_z\mathbf{F}_{az}$ and
$\mathbf{B}=-\mathbf{F}_{xy}^\dagger\mathbf{M}_{xy}\mathbf{F}_{xy}+
\mathbf{F}_{az}^\dagger\mathbf{M}_z\mathbf{F}_{az}$. The trace is then expanded
without ever forming $\mathbf{abM}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{val}=\tr\big((\mathbf{ab}\,\mathbf{ab}^\dagger\odot\mathbf{M}_t)\,\mathbf{T}\big)
=\sum_{i,j}\mathrm{ab}_i\,\big(\mathbf{M}_t\odot\mathbf{T}^{\top}\big)_{ij}\,
\overline{\mathrm{ab}_j}
=\mathrm{ab}^{\top}\big(\mathbf{M}_t\odot\mathbf{T}^{\top}\big)\overline{\mathrm{ab}},
\label{eq:trace}
\end{equation}
using $(\mathbf{X}\odot\mathbf{Y})_{ij}=\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{Y}_{ij}$ and
$\tr(\mathbf{Z})=\sum_i\mathbf{Z}_{ii}$. Blockwise, with
$\mathbf{W}_A=\mathbf{M}_{aa}\circ\mathbf{A}^{\top}$ and
$\mathbf{W}_B=\mathbf{M}_{ab}\circ\mathbf{B}^{\top}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{val}=\fa^{\top}\mathbf{W}_A\overline{\fa}+\fb^{\top}\mathbf{W}_A\overline{\fb}
+\fa^{\top}\mathbf{W}_B\overline{\fb}+\fb^{\top}\mathbf{W}_B\overline{\fa}.
\label{eq:val}
\end{equation}
The evaluation therefore needs two $2n\times2n$ Hadamard products
($\mathbf{W}_A,\mathbf{W}_B$), the four block products that form
$\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}$ in \eqref{eq:Tblock}, and four matvecs --- never
materializing the $4n\times4n$ and $3n\times3n$ matrices. This is a
$\sim20\times$ reduction in transient memory for the absorption and
stress-tensor integrals, at no change in the result (identical to the original
formula to $\sim10^{-15}$).

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

The reductions above are implemented in a C++17 library using Eigen, FFTW, and
LAPACK/BLAS. The remaining algorithmic considerations are:

\subsection{Algorithmic workflow}
\label{sec:workflow}

The implementation follows the mathematical factorization rather than treating
all layers uniformly.  The workflow is:
\begin{enumerate}
\item construct the reciprocal lattice and select the retained harmonic set;
\item form the convolution matrices only for patterned layers;
\item classify each patterned modal operator by its measured off-diagonal block
  norms;
\item solve the smallest valid eigenproblems and reconstruct the modal basis;
\item locate the trailing homogeneous suffix and evaluate it by independent
  $2\times2$ recursions;
\item compose the remaining patterned prefix with the compact suffix using
  the overlapping elimination in \eqref{eq:internal-elimination};
\item reconstruct fields and quadratic observables from the resulting modal
  amplitudes.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Parallel eigenpairs.} The two independent $n\times n$ eigenproblems
  of Sections~\ref{sec:bd}--\ref{sec:blt} are solved concurrently on two
  threads. OpenBLAS executes \texttt{zgeev} single-threaded (LAPACK-serial), so
  the pair runs $\sim1.5$--$1.8\times$ faster with bit-identical results; with a
  threaded LAPACK (e.g.\ MKL) the parallelization is disabled by a flag.
\item \emph{Star-product details.} The phase factors
  $\mathbf{d}_1,\mathbf{d}_2$ are kept as vectors and applied as diagonal
  scalings ($O(n)$ per product); matrix inversions in the star update are
  replaced by one $LU$ factorization plus two back-substitutions; the products
  $\mathbf{d}_1\mathbf{S}_{12}$ and $\mathbf{S}_{22}\mathbf{T}_{12}$ are hoisted
  as common subexpressions; and the LAPACK workspace is reused
  thread-locally.
\item \emph{Caching.} FFTW plans are cached per grid size; uniform layers with
  identical permittivity share their $(\kp,\fq,\Ph)$; identical uniform--uniform
  interface matrices are reused; and the most recent $S$-matrix is memoized.
\item \emph{Memory.} Uniform layers deduplicate their operators, and the
  block-wise post-processing of Section~\ref{sec:post} keeps the peak footprint
  bounded.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

The per-harmonic uniform-suffix factorization and the exact eigenproblem
decompositions are exact: they change only the \emph{order} of arithmetic, not
the model, and the scattering observables are bit-identical to the generic
implementation. Three caveats bound their applicability.

First, the block-lower-triangular decomposition
(Section~\ref{sec:blt}) requires the harmonic set to be the one-dimensional row
of a $y$-invariant grating, for which $\kk_y$ is constant.  For a genuinely
two-dimensional pattern at oblique incidence the upper-right block is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}_{12}=\Cmat\diag(\kk_y)\epinv\diag(\kk_x)
-\diag(\kk_x\kk_y),
\end{equation}
which does not generally vanish.  The implementation then uses the full
$2n\times2n$ problem; the choice is made from the norm of the computed block.

Second, the block-diagonal decomposition applies to the one-dimensional normal-
incidence case and to additional patterns only when the computed off-diagonal
blocks vanish.  For a general two-dimensional pattern neither spectral
reduction applies, and the full $2n\times2n$ \texttt{zgeev} remains the
recognized residual bottleneck. A matrix-free Krylov formulation
(preconditioned by the quasi-1D solves above) is the natural next step for
very large $n$, but is beyond the scope of this paper.

Third, the low-rank representation of Section~\ref{sec:binary} has not been
used to replace the dense non-Hermitian eigensolver.  Doing so requires a
stability and conditioning analysis, particularly for lossy materials and
near-degenerate modes.  It should therefore be regarded as a direction for a
reduced-order method rather than as part of the present exact algorithm.

Finally, we note that Li's factorization rules \cite{li1996fourier} and the
normal-vector method \cite{li1997formulation} improve \emph{convergence in
$n$} for high-contrast metallic gratings (reducing the required $n$), whereas
the present reductions reduce the \emph{cost per harmonic}. The two are
orthogonal and compound: every harmonic saved by better factorization is
processed at the reduced per-harmonic cost.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conc}

We have identified invariant subspaces of the finite-dimensional Maxwell
operator that remove avoidable dense algebra from Fourier-modal scattering.
Homogeneous layers form a direct sum over diffraction harmonics, and a
$y$-invariant patterned layer has an exactly reducible spectrum at both normal
and conical incidence.  These identities preserve the Fourier discretization
and its physical observables.  In the tested EUV stack, the homogeneous-suffix
algorithm reduces scattering time by $3.4$--$3.7$ for full two-dimensional
truncations; additional one-dimensional reductions produce substantially
larger gains for line gratings.  The remaining asymptotic cost is the dense
non-Hermitian modal problem of a genuinely two-dimensional patterned layer and
the final coupling of that layer to its exterior channels.  Matrix-free
boundary formulations and structure-preserving iterative eigensolvers are the
natural next steps.
